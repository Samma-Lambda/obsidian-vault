# Interest 
I am a third-year student at Willamette University pursuing degrees in Computer Science, Data Science, and Mathematics. After completing my undergraduate studies, I plan to pursue a PhD in cryptography, number theory, or quantum algorithms. Because Willamette is a low-research institution, I am applying to this program both to gain the experience needed to be a competitive graduate applicant and because I would genuinely love to spend my summer studying quantum algorithms and cryptography.

My interest in these fields began in my abstract algebra and number theory courses. After finishing number theory, I asked my professor how I could continue studying the subject in a more applied setting. She pointed me toward cryptography and quantum algorithms because of my background in abstract algebra and computer science, and recommended that I read _Quantum Computing Explained_ by David McMahon. Over the next few months, I worked my way through the book and implemented each algorithm as I went. I chose not to use qiskit, since it hid too much of the underlying mathematics for my liking. Instead, I used NumPy to simulate qubits as vectors, gates as matrices, and multi-qubit systems through the Kronecker product. I thoroughly enjoyed this process and have since implemented the Deutsch–Jozsa, Quantum Fourier Transform, Phase Estimation, and Shor’s algorithms. Due to computational limits, I am unable to simulate more than 10 qubits. If you are interested in seeing my work this project is publicly available and left a link to my personal projects below. 

Outside of quantum computing, I have also enjoyed implementing cryptographic algorithms. For my final project in number theory, I wrote a seven-page primer on ECC and implemented both its symmetric and asymmetric schemes in Python. I enjoyed the project enough that the following summer I implemented two more cryptographic algorithms: RSA and lattice-based encryption. These projects are also linked below. 

While quantum algorithms and cryptography have many lucrative applications, my motivation for studying them is not financial. I enjoy them because they apply theoretical mathematics in a practical setting and give me a chance to use my programming skills. I love how tensors and number theory are not just useful but nessacary to understand algorithms, and how it feels slightly like science fiction to use a high-dimensional lattices for secure communication. I know that implementing these algorithms is difficult, and understanding them is even harder, but I genuinely enjoy the process.

Thank you for your time and consideration. I would be grateful for the opportunity to be part of this program.

# Career Goals 
No matter where I go in life, I hope to find others who share my love of math.

When I was still in high school, touring Willamette University, I walked past a group of students laughing while working on the most complicated math I had ever seen. They weren't off topic, but instead were laughing at a silly approach someone had proposed to solving a problem. I remember being so shocked they weren't treating math like a chore they were forced to do, but instead a mental frolic. To them a difficult question wasn't a terrifying task, but instead a mental mountain you get to climb with your friends and absurd amounts of caffeine. 

I wanted to join them, I committed to Willamette and eagerly joined the math department. I was not disappointed as the mindset I had seen while touring remained when I joined. The professors and students continued to show me that math could be a fun mental adventure, but also required diligence and care. Once I had taken the introductory math courses, I remember feeling like a little kid who finally got to play with the big kids when I took my first upper level math class(Number Theory!). 

Since then I have fallen deeply in love with mathematics. Whenever I have a break from school, or need to relax I will either read a little of a textbook or find youtube lectures online. This self study of mathematics is not incredibly rigorous, I often learn more like a tourist strolling through and seeing the pretty sites rather then engaging deeply, but it is still rewarding  nonetheless. I chose to self studied abstract algebra over my freshman year summer and got invited to join Abstract Algebra 3 despite never taking abstract algebra 1 or 2. I self studied algebraic topology over my sophomore year summer, and was able to apply the skills that I learned on my biophysics research by doing topological data analysis on filament networks. 

So considering the fact that all I want to do is more math, and I don't have any particularly strong desire to be wealthy. I plan on pursing a PhD with plans on becoming a professor. I would love to study Quantum Algorithms or Cryptography as that is what I found to enjoy, but would be happy studying pretty much any field of math. 

I have done a wide variety of research to prepare for graduate school.  In the summer of 2024, I joined my university’s Summer Research Program as a full-cycle developer. I designed a web platform that used machine learning to generate optimally challenging study questions and integrated the OpenAI API to incorporate large language models. This project earned Best Poster Presentation at the MURDOC Conference and resulted in a publication in ACM SIGCSE 2025.
Over the last year and a half, I have served as a data scientist on an NSF-funded sustainability language project. I curated a large corpus of text from Twitter, ESG reports, surveys, and prior studies, and trained a machine learning classifier that achieved a 98.5 F1 score in detecting sustainable language. My contributions included building the classifier, engineering additional features to improve performance, and creating a pipeline to monitor data drift using informational divergence. We are currently writing the paper with plans to submit it for publication. 
This last summer I participated in the Computational Applied Mathematics Program REU at California Polytechnic State University, where I studied linear programming for truck-and-drone delivery scheduling. I developed the team’s mixed-integer linear programming model, evaluated optimization constraints, and co-authored a 30-page technical report, for which I prepared the LaTeX manuscript.
Finally this last semster In my biophysics work, I collaborated with a small team of biologists and physicists studying myosin VI using gliding filament assays. As the sole computer scientist on the project, I was responsible for building and applying tools to analyze filament video data. I modified ATLAS’s convolutional neural networks to more effectively track dense actin filament networks and developed an autoencoder to mitigate photobleaching effects over time. I also applied topological data analysis to uncover structural patterns in filament motion, identifying distinct void-size distributions under varying ATP concentrations. I have worked on this project for six months, largely independently, while consulting teammates and my computer vision professor as needed.
