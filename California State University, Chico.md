###### Why are you interested in this program? What are your mathematical interests?
I am interested in this program because it focuses on algebra and number theory, which align closely with my academic interests, and it also includes graph theory and combinatorics. While I have taken several courses in algebra and number theory, I have not had the opportunity to study graph theory and combinatorics beyond basic counting techniques in probability courses. This program would allow me to strengthen my background in my primary areas of interest while also learning material that I have not yet had the chance to study formally.

My mathematical interests are mainly in abstract algebra and number theory. I first became interested in these subjects during the summer after my freshman year, when I knew I would be taking my first upper level number theory course. To prepare, I contacted the professor teaching the course and asked for suggestions. She recommended reading _Contemporary Abstract Algebra_ by Joseph Gallian, which I read over the summer and supplemented with wrath of math youtube videos. 

When I took the number theory class, this preparation made it easier to follow the material and understand topic such as ideals. I genuinely enjoyed this course, with its being my favorite math class that I have taken in my undergraduate. I specifically enjoyed clever applications of Lagranges theorem and proving the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity. Often I would meet with the professor during her office hours and discuss the connections between abstract algebra and number theory.  


After that semester, I was invited to enroll in an Abstract Algebra III course despite not having taken Abstract Algebra I or II by the same professor. In that course, I studied modules, algebras, tensor products, and introductory algebraic geometry. I am still taking the follow up of these courses to date, being currently enrolled in Abstract Algebra V. These classes cemented that I want to pursue a PhD after completing my undergraduate degree, hopefully studying number theory or algebraic geometry. 

While I have continued to build my background in algebra, I have had limited opportunities to continue studying number theory beyond my previous coursework. This program would either give me the opportunity to continue studying algebra within combinatorics, or give me a chance to learn more about number theory which I enjoyed but have not had a chance to revisit. 

Additionally I have yet to find an area of mathematics I have not enjoyed, and would love to spend my summer learning about and doing math, no mater what the topic. 

###### What is the deepest, most exciting, or most interesting mathematical result or application that you have ever learned about? What did you find compelling about it?
I really liked thinking about this question, because there are so many mathematical results which are exciting and profound. The two that stand out the most to me are Lagrange's Theorem and the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity. Though there are two close contenders of the classification of compact, path connected, triangulable 2 manifolds for its super intuitive proof, or the central limit theorem because it proves something profound with almost nothing in the supposition. 

Lagrange’s Theorem isn’t especially complicated, but it completely changed how I thought about algebra when I first learned it. At the time, I was self-studying group theory from _Contemporary Abstract Algebra_ and was floundering. I understood the definitions, but I didn’t really see why any of it mattered. I remember reading the theorem while sitting on the deck outside my hotel room during a family vacation, and immediately seeing how useful the result was. The idea that knowing the size of a group, immediately tells you all possible sizes of its subgroups is an insane result to me. It made me understand that algebra was about proving results for incredibly general structures. 

I had a similar experience with quadratic reciprocity in my number theory class. We had been working with Legendre symbols for a while and proving results case by case, which was interesting but also felt a bit messy. Additionally we had worked a lot with a lot with complex extensions of the integers, mostly to find solutions to elliptic curves. Seeing both of these come together to create such a useful theorem was incredible. I am also biased because I was currently working on a final project for that class on elliptic curve cryptography and it massively sped up my algorithms. 

Overall I think the results I have found most profound in mathematics have been theorems which prove general useful statements with small suppositions. These theorems help me understand the underlying concepts more intuitively, taking groups from sets with a binary operation to a structure with cool emergent properties.
