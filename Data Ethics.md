I was really tired of reading about and talking about AI, so I decided to do my deep dive on under what conditions the government can get data from companies.

I started by literally googling that question and I quickly found a post talking about this topic by the Brennan Center for Justice, a nonprofit organization that focuses on law education and policy issues. According to this website, the Fourth Amendment (which prevents unlawful search and seizure) does not always extend to data that you willingly give up to a company. This extends to things like telephone metadata and most of the data that apps collect about your usage, since you technically chose to provide that information when using the service.

This rule comes from what is sometimes called the “third-party doctrine,” where courts say that once you share information with a company, you lose some expectation of privacy in it. So if a phone company or social media platform stores certain records about you, the government may be able to request them with less strict requirements than if the data were kept entirely private by you. Additionally companies can sell anonymized or aggregated data, things like average expenditure or time spent on the app. This may not seem initially bad but can lead to problems. Even apps like period trackers and mental health apps are able to sell your data aggregated because they are not "technically" medical records. 

However, this does not fully extend to what courts sometimes call “sensitive data.” For example, more detailed location data showing where you were when you made calls or sent texts can be treated differently because it reveals a lot about your daily life and movements. In some cases, the government needs stronger legal justification, like a warrant, to access that type of information.

But there is an important complication. Even if the government cannot directly seize certain data, a company can sometimes sell or voluntarily share that data with other parties. Because the company technically owns the database it created, the issue shifts away from pure constitutional law and more into consumer rights and contract law. If that data is sold or shared, it can end up in datasets that government agencies might later obtain through legal or commercial channels.

This makes the whole issue less about search and seizure in the classic sense and more about what rights consumers actually have over their own data. I think that people should have the right to their own data, and that the definition of sensitive data needs to be expanded.
