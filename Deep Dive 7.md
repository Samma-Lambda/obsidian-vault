Inspired by class on Tuesday, I decided to do my deep dive on centaur chess. This is a format where a human chess player and a computer work together so that the pair can perform better than either a single computer or a single human on their own.

The idea was originally introduced by Garry Kasparov, who had a long history of playing matches against chess engines. In these centaur chess competitions the computers were restricted in a few ways. They were not allowed to access large databases of past chess games. Instead, the computer’s job was mainly to search through the tree of possible moves and evaluate whether the move the human was considering might lead to a blunder.

The computer was also given a limited amount of computation time. For example, it might only have an hour of compute available for the entire game. Because of this, the human player had to decide when it was worth spending that computation. If the position seemed critical they could use more of the computer’s time, while in simpler positions they might save it.

What makes this interesting is that the human and computer are not really playing chess together in the usual sense. Instead, the human is deciding how to allocate the computer’s resources during the game. According to players who participated in these matches, this setup made it extremely hard to blunder pieces. Many of the simple tactical mistakes that humans usually make were caught by the computer, which let them play something closer to their ideal version of the game.

This made me curious about how computers perform against humans in other games, especially asymmetrical games or games that involve lying. Chess sometimes has a little bit of bluffing, but not nearly as much as something like poker.

It is much harder to rank poker players because the game is very inconsistent and depends heavily on chance in the short term. There are ranking systems like the Global Poker Index, but these rankings often depend a lot on how frequently someone plays rather than just how strong they are. Since a computer can play thousands of games compared to the number a person can play, this type of ranking system does not really work for comparing humans and bots.

To try to evaluate computer performance more directly, researchers organized a match between four top professional poker players and one of the strongest poker bots at the time, called Claudico. In the end the humans actually beat Claudico by a noticeable margin. Many of the players said that the bot did not behave like an experienced poker player. For example, it would sometimes make very strange bets, like betting $19,000 to win a pot of only $700. According to experts, a single experiment like this is not enough to determine whether the bot is truly worse than humans, but the event has not really been repeated under the same conditions.

Overall, looking into this topic made me realize that in symmetric games like chess it is relatively easy to determine whether a computer is better than people. In games that involve uncertainty, psychology, and probabilistic reasoning, it becomes much harder and requires more advanced statistics to evaluate performance.

It also raises an interesting question about who these bots are actually for. I do not think many people are particularly interested in playing poker against robots, and even chess engines are often intentionally weakened when people play against them. In some ways these systems might mostly exist for researchers and enthusiasts who build stronger and stronger algorithms and then compete to see whose bot performs the best.
